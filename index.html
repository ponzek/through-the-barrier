Classical expectation

Should the particle get through, according to classical mechanics?

Classical T—
Quantum T—

Classical idea. A particle either reflects when E < V₀ or passes when E > V₀, so T is exactly 0 or 1. Move E across V₀, then compare that with the quantum value.

Important. This plot shows potential energy. The barrier is not a literal wall; it is a region where the particle would need more energy in classical mechanics.

Act II · The mechanism
2

Quantum wavefunction

What changes when the particle is a quantum scattering state?

φ = 0.00 π

What you are seeing. The solid line is one phase of the wavefunction, Re[ψ(x)e−iφ]. The shaded envelope is |ψ(x)|, the part that shows how large the wave can be at each position.

Not a path. The animation changes the phase of a stationary state. It does not show a particle traveling across the screen, and R and T do not change with the animation.

3

Relative probability density

How does the magnitude of the quantum state vary across the barrier?

How to read it. Higher |ψ|² means the wave has more relative presence there. Left of the barrier, incoming and reflected waves interfere; right of the barrier, the flat level equals T.

Relative scale. These scattering states are not normalizable like a bound electron, so |ψ|² is relative density with incident amplitude set to 1.

4

Reflection versus transmission

Out of 100 simulated particles, how many reflect from the barrier and how many pass through?

Plain English. “Reflect” means the simulated particle is predicted to turn around at the barrier. It does not mean any dataset was sent back to Quantum Gatekeeper.

Model check. The formula and wavefunction solver still run in the background. The side panel tells you if they match.

Tunneling does not cost energy. A transmitted particle leaves with the same E it arrived with; only the probability of transmission is reduced.

Numerical example & boundary checks

Does the implementation reproduce known physics?

Model, variables, and assumptions

Time-independent Schrödinger equation for an electron (m = 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg) on a rectangular barrier V(x) = V₀ for 0 ≤ x ≤ a, else 0. κ = √(2m(V₀−E))/ħ;  T = [1 + V₀² sinh²(κa) / (4E(V₀−E))]⁻¹ for E < V₀;  T = [1 + V₀² sin²(qa) / (4E(E−V₀))]⁻¹ for E > V₀;  T = [1 + mV₀a²/(2ħ²)]⁻¹ at E = V₀;  R + T = 1.
